      Intro week · deck “Week 1 SCOR DS” · Quiz 1

      SCOR Digital Standard (SCOR DS)

      The Supply Chain Operations Reference model, maintained by ASCM (Association for Supply Chain Management). A free, open-access, fully digital standard that gives organizations a common language for describing, measuring and improving supply chains. It is a reference model, not software and not a step-by-step method.

      What makes SCOR DS different

      • Open access: free under Creative Commons, so small suppliers can use it too.
      • Fully digital: navigable online by process, metric, practice or skill.
      • Sustainability built in: environmental and social standards are in the core model.
      • Network, not chain: a synchronous network with Orchestrate at the centre.

      History (anchor dates)

      1996Supply Chain Council founded; first SCOR model
      2000sVersions add metrics, practices, Return and Enable
      2014Supply Chain Council merges with APICS
      2017SCOR 12.0, last version of the classic model
      2019APICS rebrands as ASCM
      2022SCOR Digital Standard launched (a redesign, not “version 13”)

      Four building blocks

      BlockWhat it isQuestion it answers
      ProcessesStandard descriptions of management processes and how they relateWhat is our process?
      PerformanceStandard metrics grouped by attributes, linked to strategyHow well does it perform?
      PracticesManagement practices that produce significantly better performanceHow could it be done better?
      PeopleStandard skills, experience and training with proficiency levelsWho has the skills?

      Hierarchy of detail

      • Level 1, process types: scope and top-level processes. Strategic targets are set here.
      • Level 2, process categories: configuration, the variants (stocked vs make-to-order).
      • Level 3, process elements: steps, inputs, outputs, metrics and practices. Most diagnosis and benchmarking happens here.
      • Level 4, implementation: company-specific activities and systems. Outside the standard.

      The seven processes

      • Orchestrate: the integrating layer. Business rules, enterprise planning, HR, network design, technology, data & analytics, contracts & compliance, risk, ESG & circular supply chain.
      • Plan: operational roadmaps; requirements, resources, capacity, gaps. Executed across all processes.
      • Order: customer purchase activities: locate products, pricing & payment, fulfilment status, order data.
      • Source: operational procurement: order from suppliers, schedule deliveries, receive & verify.
      • Transform: production scheduling, manufacturing, assembly, maintenance & repair, services.
      • Fulfill: schedule deliveries, pick, pack, ship, install, invoice.
      • Return: diagnose, evaluate entitlement, decide disposition, feed back into Source and Plan.

      Classic SCOR 12.0 → SCOR DS

      SCOR 12.0SCOR DSWhat changed

      PlanPlanExplicitly executed across all processes

      Performance attributes: three lenses

      CategoryAttributeMeaningExample Level 1 metric
      ResilienceReliabilityRight product, place, time, condition, documentationPerfect order fulfillment
      ResponsivenessSpeed of performing tasksOrder fulfillment cycle time
      AgilityAbility to respond to external changeSC adaptability (upside/downside)
      EconomicCostCost of operating the processesTotal SC management cost
      ProfitFinancial benefit realizedSC contribution to margin
      AssetsEfficiency of asset useCash-to-cash cycle time
      SustainabilityEnvironmentalResource use, emissions, wasteGHG emissions; energy, water, waste
      SocialWorkforce, communities, ethicsTraining, wages, diversity

      No supply chain is best at everything: pick the attributes to lead on and stay at parity on the rest.

      Metric decomposition

      Perfect order fulfillment (L1)→In fullTo commit dateDocumentation accuracyPerfect condition

      If delivery to commit date drops, drill into L3: forecast accuracy (Plan), supplier on-time (Source), schedule adherence (Transform), pick & ship cycle time (Fulfill). Symptom at L1 → root cause at L3 → remedy through practices → capability through people.

      Practices, people, applying it

      • Standard practices: baseline (ABC classification, cycle counting). Best: proven (integrated S&OP, VMI). Emerging: new, tech-enabled (AI demand sensing).
      • People proficiency: Novice → Beginner → Competent → Proficient → Expert.
      • Five-stage program: Pre-SCOR (business case, sponsor, team) → Set the scope → Configure (map, benchmark, gaps) → Optimize (practices, projects) → Implement, then re-measure.
      • SCORmark = ASCM benchmarking against peers. ASCM-reported benefits: 2–6× first-year ROI, up to 3% of sales, 30% faster transformations (vendor-reported).
      • Avoid: mapping everything to Level 4 before acting, treating SCOR as an IT project, chasing every metric, copying “best practices” without context, ignoring sustainability and Return.
      Most likely on the quizName the 7 processes; Enable→Orchestrate, Make→Transform, Deliver→Order + Fulfill; the 4 building blocks; the 3 attribute categories and what sits in each; L3 is where diagnosis happens; place an activity (pricing = Order, pick/pack = Fulfill, repair = Transform, disposition = Return).

      Introduction to Supply Chain Technology

      Supply chain technology: the tools, systems, platforms and digital capabilities used to plan, execute, monitor and improve supply chain activities. It supports physical flows and information flows. Effective technology solves a business problem; it does not just add sophistication.

      Why it matters

      • Competition plays out across supply chains on speed, visibility, resilience and customer experience.
      • Connects partners and drives information flow; real-time information supports faster decisions.
      • Automation improves productivity, quality, safety and consistency; digital capabilities create new business models.

      The three-tier digital journey

      DigitizationDigitalizationDigital transformation
      DefinitionConverting physical/analog information to digital formUsing digital tools to improve an existing processRedesigning processes, business model and value proposition around digital tech
      Invoice exampleScanning paper invoicesAutomated invoice processingRedesigned procure-to-pay ecosystem

      SMAC, big data, M2M & IoT

      • Social · Mobile · Analytics · Cloud. The cloud is the core, mobile devices are the endpoints (edge), social networks connect them.
      • Megatrends: connectivity, cloud services (resources on demand, no need to own them), big data & analytics.
      • Big data: high-volume, mostly text, 80–90% unstructured; from sensors, social media, clickstream.
      • M2M: sensor-embedded products share real-time data via radio. IoT: capabilities enabled when physical things connect to the internet via sensors.

      Data → information → knowledge → wisdom

      Data: recorded raw facts→Information: data in context→Knowledge: expertise applied to a problem→Wisdom: moral code + experience to judge

      Types of information systems

      SystemUserKey points
      TPS transaction processingOperationalInternal (payroll, purchases) and external (customers, suppliers) transactions. Batch: collect, then process; cheaper but can be out of date. OLTP: each transaction in real time; costs more (always-on systems, more computing, more controls).
      MISManagersReports for monitoring and control: periodic (schedule), exception (only when outside parameters), ad hoc (on demand).
      DSSAnalysts, managersInteractive, model-based; what-if and goal-seeking; data from many sources.
      EISExecutivesStrategic; long-term trends; weakly structured internal + external data.

      Building blocks & supply chain data

      • Building blocks: data, connectivity, cloud, automation, analytics & AI, integration.
      • Master: products, suppliers, customers, locations. Transactional: orders, receipts, shipments, invoices. Operational: inventory, capacity, equipment status. External: weather, traffic, markets, social signals.
      • Data quality determines the reliability of technology-enabled decisions.

      The technology landscape

      AreaTechnologies
      PlanningForecasting, S&OP, demand planning, MRP, optimization
      OperationsMES, robotics, IoT, digital twins
      TransportationTMS, telematics, route optimization
      Procuremente-Procurement, supplier portals, spend analytics
      WarehousingWMS, RFID, AMRs, automated storage & retrieval
      Customer interfaceCRM, order management, e-commerce
      • ERP integrates core processes; strength: cross-functional integration; challenge: complexity, process change, data quality, cost.
      • WMS KPIs: inventory accuracy, order cycle time, pick rate, utilization. TMS: carriers, loads, routes, tracking.
      • EDI stays important in B2B even as APIs grow (deeply embedded, costly to switch); APIs complement it. APIs need governance, security, standards.
      • RFID: no line of sight needed; consider tag cost, infrastructure, privacy.
      • Cloud: scalable and fast; risks are cybersecurity, availability, data governance, vendor dependence.
      • AI / ML: supervised = labeled outcomes; unsupervised = finds structure. Models drift as demand changes, so they need ongoing monitoring and retraining.
      • Robotics & cobots: cobots work alongside people in selected tasks, but still need process redesign and safety analysis. AMRs need little fixed infrastructure.
      • Additive manufacturing: local production, spare parts on demand, less inventory.
      • Blockchain: makes recorded data hard to alter, but can't guarantee it was right when entered (garbage in, garbage out). Needs network participation.
      • Digital twin: digital representation of a physical asset/process linked to live data; what-if analysis.
      • 5G & edge: edge processes data close to where it is generated when latency matters.

      Analytics, visibility, risk

      Descriptive: what happened?Diagnostic: why?Predictive: what is likely?Prescriptive: what should we do?
      • Visibility needs data that is timely, accurate and actionable. More data ≠ better visibility.
      • Cybersecurity: controls are identity & access, encryption, monitoring, backups, incident response. Third-party cyber is part of SC risk management.
      • Adoption challenges: cost, legacy systems, resistance and skills gaps, cyber/privacy, weak business case or sponsor, scaling pilots.

      Technology selection framework

      1 Define the problem→2 Objectives & KPIs→3 Map current processes→4 Evaluate alternatives & integration→5 Cost, risk, skills, vendor→6 Pilot, measure, scale
      The mock quiz answer key words it slightly differently1 Define the business problem and objectives · 2 Assess the current state · 3 Identify alternatives · 4 Evaluate alternatives (cost, fit, integration, ROI) · 5 Pilot and implement in phases · 6 Monitor, measure and scale. Either way, defining the problem always comes first, and evaluating alternatives comes later.

      Business case: total cost of ownership, not purchase price; judge payback, ROI, strategic fit and feasibility.

      Smart warehouse case (manual picking, paper, picking errors, labour shortage): roadmap WMS + barcode/RFID, then AMRs and analytics. KPIs: picking accuracy, lines picked per labour hour, inventory record accuracy, order cycle time / OTIF, labour cost per order. Risks: integration and data quality, resistance and training gaps, go-live disruption, premature automation, cyber and vendor dependence.

      Digital Supply Chain Strategy & Transformation

      Core principle: strategy drives technology, never the reverse. Business strategy → SC strategy → technology strategy → implementation.

      Environment & strategy basics

      • Internal environment: structure, culture, climate. Task environment: industry forces and stakeholders you deal with daily. General environment: PESTEL / CREST forces, needs environmental scanning.
      • Strategy: long-term goals, courses of action and resource allocation. Successful if it gives sustainable competitive advantage.
      • Process: vision/mission/objectives → analyze environment (SWOT) → formulate (corporate, business, functional) → implement (leadership, culture, HR, information & control systems) → assess.

      Porter's Five Forces & how SCM influences them

      ForceSCM leverExample
      New entrantsRaise barriers to entryReal-time inventory, long-term supplier and logistics relationships
      Buyer powerRaise switching costsFaster fulfilment, localized assortments from demand data
      Supplier powerIncrease alternativesMulti-sourcing, supplier benchmarking
      SubstitutesLower willingness to switchBetter quality, competitive price, faster delivery
      RivalryLower cost, differentiate, standardizeRapid response to demand, efficient processes

      Why Five Forces + Value Chain matter for digital strategy: they show where SC capabilities and technology can influence competitive forces and create value. They don't pick the cheapest tech or guarantee advantage.

      Value chain → systems: ERP → everything (backbone); WMS → inbound logistics & operations; TMS → outbound logistics; CRM → marketing, sales, service; AI/analytics → margin by removing waste.

      Red ocean vs blue ocean

      Red ocean (exploit)Blue ocean (explore / disrupt)
      Compete in an existing market for existing demandCreate a new market and new demand
      Beat the competition; low cost OR differentiationMake competition irrelevant; value innovation: low cost AND differentiation
      Banking, gas stations, autos, Nike vs AdidasCirque du Soleil, iTunes, Air Jordan, Nike's digital ecosystem

      Digital supply chain

      • Connected data, analytics and automation replacing static, batch processes with real-time, self-adjusting ones.
      • Building blocks: smart factories + smart warehouses + smart logistics, powered by cloud, AI, analytics.
      • Drivers: market, operational, information, risk & sustainability, technology.
      • Tech stack in order: IoT sensors capture → AI/ML decides → Cloud distributes → Robotics execute → Blockchain audit trail.

      SC strategy types

      TypeGoalTrades offDemand / settingExamples
      EfficientCost: lean, standardized, high utilization, low inventoryFlexibilityStable, predictableWalmart, Costco, IKEA
      ResponsiveSpeed & service: short lead times, rapid replenishmentCostVolatile, short life cyclesNike, Zara, Apple launches
      ResilientReliability & continuity: redundancy, multiple suppliersEfficiencyDisruption-proneHealthcare, semiconductors
      SustainableEnvironmental & socialShort-term profitRising regulationPatagonia, IKEA, Nike “Move to Zero”

      Lean (cost leaders: Toyota, Walmart) vs Agile (differentiators: Nike, Zara) vs Hybrid (lean upstream + agile downstream: Dell build-to-order).

      Maturity models

      1 Visibility→2 Connectivity→3 Predictive intelligence→4 Autonomous & adaptive
      • Sequential in principle (no skipping clean data) but rarely a straight line; maturity can be uneven: stage 4 in one region, stage 1 in another.
      • A firm with good transactions but fragmented data should build connectivity before expecting AI prediction to work.
      • Gartner model: 5 levels, assessed on Data, Process, Technology, Talent, Culture.
      • Transformation journey: Diagnose → Pilot → Scale → Transform → Optimize.

      Orchestrate & the control tower

      • Orchestrate = the brain of SCOR DS; strategic and decision-driving. It sets the planning framework; Plan executes it.
      • The control tower is the primary technology enabling Orchestrate: real-time, end-to-end visibility that collects, synthesizes and acts (more than a dashboard).
      • Decisions: operational (supplier 3 days late), tactical (demand +20% in Ontario, expedite from Calgary?), strategic (which nodes are most vulnerable?).
      • Principle: Seeing → Understanding → Deciding → Acting. Layers: visibility → analytics → action.
      • P&G: “drowning in data but starved for insights” → control tower; a capability for “winning at the shelf”.
      • Ecosystem thinking: no firm digitizes alone.

      Nike case

      • Thai Hua Rubber (Thailand) → Pou Chen (Vietnam) → Memphis DC → Sport Chek, West Edmonton Mall → you.
      • 2017 “Triple Double”: 2X Direct (30,000 retailers → ~40 partners), 2X Speed (RFID, halved creation time), 2X Innovation (app data + Celect AI).
      • 2020 Consumer Direct Acceleration: 50% digital revenue target by FY2025; regional hubs; ML replenishment (7% of SKUs = 40% of demand); Nike Fit.
      • RFID tags = digitization; AI forecasting = digitalization; wholesale → DTC ecosystem = transformation.
      Most likely on the quizMatch a scenario to efficient/responsive/resilient/sustainable; transformation vs digitization; tech stack order (IoT → AI → Cloud → Robotics); why Five Forces + Value Chain matter; uneven maturity; maturity stage order; control tower decision levels; red vs blue ocean (OR vs AND).

      Supply Chain Risk, Resilience & Sustainability

      Built from the mock quiz and related slidesThe Topic 3 slides haven't been uploaded yet. These notes come from the mock quiz answer key (which quotes this lesson) and the risk and sustainability material in the other decks. Upload the Topic 3 deck to fill in the rest.

      Risk, disruption, crisis

      Risk: a possible event→Disruption: the event interrupts flows→Crisis: consequences severe enough to threaten the wider business
      • The lesson separates them by the nature and severity of the event. A disruption can become a crisis when its consequences are severe enough.
      • SC uncertainty: lack of predictability about future flows of materials, information, money and capacity (inaccurate forecasts, variable lead times, late shipments, price swings).
      • Internal risks (planning and control) vs external risks (demand, supply, regulatory, partner, ethical). Sources: pandemics, geopolitics, weather, cyberattacks, labour, supplier failure.

      What resilience is (and isn't)

      • Resilience does not mean preventing every disruption. It means limiting the impact, recovering quickly and adapting.
      • More suppliers ≠ automatically more resilient: diversification helps but adds procurement complexity, supplier-management work and cost.
      • More inventory ≠ automatically more resilient: excess stock brings carrying costs and obsolescence risk.
      • Connectivity cuts both ways: digital connections raise visibility and cybersecurity exposure.
      • Technology helps (monitoring, scenario planning, digital twins, multi-tier visibility, rapid reconfiguration) but complements, not replaces, risk management and relationships.

      How efficiency creates vulnerability

      Efficiency practiceBenefitVulnerability it creates
      Single or limited sourcingLower purchasing cost, volume leverageExposure if that supplier fails
      JIT / minimal inventoryLower carrying costFew buffers during a disruption
      High capacity utilizationEfficient asset useLittle spare capacity to absorb shocks
      Global sourcingLower unit costGeopolitical, transport and environmental exposure
      Long, lean networksEconomies of scaleLonger recovery times

      The goal is not to eliminate efficiency but to balance efficiency, risk and resilience.

      Cybersecurity & governance

      • Attack surfaces: ransomware, compromised suppliers, stolen credentials, data leakage. Partners often have access to your systems, so third-party cyber risk is part of SC risk management.
      • Data governance: who owns, accesses, changes and uses data; explainable, monitored AI.

      Sustainability

      • Triple Bottom Line: economic (profit), social (people), environmental (planet), evaluated at the same time.
      • Unintended consequences: a “green” change in one place can shift harm elsewhere in the life cycle (eco packaging that needs more energy to make or burdens transport).
      • SCOR DS: environmental and social attributes sit beside cost and reliability; ESG and circularity live in Orchestrate; Return closes the loop.
      • Trade-offs: air freight improves responsiveness but worsens emissions. Hardest to measure: scope 3 emissions and social metrics deep in the supplier base.
      Most likely on the quizResilience = limit impact, recover, adapt (not prevent everything); “more suppliers / more inventory automatically = resilient” is false; connectivity raises visibility and cyber exposure; disruption → crisis; TBL; unintended consequences; efficiency practices and their trade-offs (essay).

      Enterprise Supply Chain Systems 1

      Enterprise SC system: an integrated technology environment supporting end-to-end processes across functions, units and partners. Not standalone. Supports transactions, planning & execution, and monitoring & decisions.

      Six flows & scope

      • Information (bidirectional), demand, product (incl. reverse), value, financial (terms, consignment, title), risk.
      • Systems mainly manage information flow, and through it control the others.
      • Scope: Source → Make → Store → Move → Sell → Return. (Cycle counting = Store; carrier selection = Move; allocation & invoicing = Sell.)
      • Integration doesn't automatically create good decisions: bad processes and data move fast too.

      The bullwhip effect

      • Small consumer-demand changes become larger order swings upstream. Each stage holds more inventory; longer chains are worse. P&G Pampers.
      • Four causes (Lee, Padmanabhan & Whang 1997): demand forecast updating, order batching, price fluctuations, rationing & shortage gaming.
      • Each tier acts rationally; it only sees the orders of the tier below.
      • Remedies: share POS data, plan more often, stable pricing (EDLP), shorter lead times (cross-docking, JIT, EDI), VMI and CPFR.

      MRP to cloud

      1960s–70sMRP: explode the BOM against the master schedule. Assumes infinite capacity.
      1980sMRP II: adds capacity planning, shop floor control, costing.
      1990sERP: single integrated database (SAP R/3, Oracle; Y2K wave).
      2000sSCM suites: APS and best-of-breed execution on top of ERP.
      2010s–nowCloud: SaaS, real-time visibility, AI planning, networks.

      Landscape

      LayerSystemsRole
      Customer-facingOMS, CRM, e-commerceCapture demand
      PlanningDemand planning, S&OP/IBP, APS, inventory optimizationDecide what should happen
      ExecutionWMS, TMS, MESMake it happen
      System of recordERPRecord what did happen
      IntegrationEDI, APIs, iPaaSPlumbing
      Across allAnalytics & control towerVisibility, alerts, KPIs
      • ERP strengths: one version of the truth, auditable (SOX), real-time financial impact. Limits: transactional not optimizing, rigid, inward-looking.
      • One goods receipt raises inventory, marks the PO received, records the liability for three-way match and updates the GL.
      • Planning horizons: strategic (years) → tactical (months) → operational (weeks/days). Multi-echelon optimization lowers total inventory for the same service.
      • MES tracks OEE; TMS freight audit is often the quickest ROI.
      • EDI 850 PO · 856 ASN · 810 invoice; ANSI X12 (N. America), EDIFACT (international). GTIN = number behind the barcode.

      Cases & success factors

      CaseWhat happenedLesson
      Hershey 1999ERP + CRM + planning big-bang go-live before HalloweenNo big-bang go-live in peak season
      Nike 2000–01i2 demand planning over- and under-orderedPilot, test with real data, human oversight
      Target Canada 2013–15Bad master data: empty shelves, full DCsMaster data quality is foundational

      Success factors: executive sponsorship, process before technology, data quality & governance, change management, phased rollout & testing, benefits tracking. TradeLens (shut 2023): tech alone fails without partner participation.

      Enterprise Systems 2: Beyond ERP

      ERP is the backbone, not the whole skeleton. ERP is the system of record at the core; specialized systems (APS, e-procurement/SRM, CRM/OMS, WMS, TMS, MES, SCEM) at the edge add optimization, execution detail and real-time visibility, connected by integration.

      Why go beyond ERP

      ERP does wellERP gaps
      • System of record for transactions
      • Shared master data
      • Financial control (GL, AP, AR, costing)
      • Standard P2P, O2C, plan-to-produce
      • Audit trail & compliance
      • Simplified planning (infinite capacity, fixed lead times, one plan)
      • Batch, not real-time
      • Thin execution detail (bins, routing, machines)
      • Inside one company only
      • Weak advanced analytics
      • New requirements: sustainability data, omnichannel promising
      • Costly customization
      NeedSystemExample of what ERP can't do
      Network-wide optimizationAPS / SCMPlan against finite capacity
      Bin-level warehouse executionWMSERP knows 500 units are “in the Toronto DC”; WMS knows bin A-14-03
      Carrier rating, routing, freight auditTMSBuild multi-stop loads
      Real-time shop-floor controlMESERP plans in days; production happens in seconds
      Customer & supplier engagementCRM / SRMManage relationships, not just records
      Live exception visibilitySCEM / control towerERP says what was recorded; SCEM says what's going wrong now

      Suite (one vendor): one data model, simpler upgrades, can be shallower. Best-of-breed: deepest functionality, more interfaces. Most firms end up hybrid.

      Systems mapped to SCOR DS

      ProcessSystems
      OrchestrateSCEM / control tower, master data management, iPaaS, risk & performance analytics
      PlanAPS / SCM planning, S&OP tools, demand sensing
      OrderCRM, OMS, ATP/CTP engines

      Integration: how systems talk

      ArchitectureConnectionsPros / cons
      Point-to-pointn(n−1)/2 (8 systems → 28)Quick first link; brittle “spaghetti”, every upgrade breaks links, no central monitoring
      Middleware hub≈ n (8 → 8)Translate, route, log, retry; change one system, fix one link; hub becomes critical

      Master data & TCO

      • Bad master data breaks good systems. One wrong unit of measure: MRP orders 12× too much/little, billing at the wrong price, WMS slots pallets that don't fit, TMS under-estimates weight and cube. Target Canada: 133 stores, $2B+ losses, CCAA in 2015.
      • Validation rules: Completeness (every item has supplier, lead time, price) · Validity (lead time > 0, approved UoM) · Consistency (same code means the same in ERP, WMS, TMS) · Reasonableness (flag lead time 3× the family average, price change >20%) · Uniqueness (no duplicate suppliers).
      • Data governance is a supply chain capability, not an IT chore.
      • TCO iceberg: licence/subscription is the tip; below: implementation, integration, data cleansing & migration, customization, training & change, hosting, support & upgrades.
      • Build (max fit, slow, maintain forever; only for true differentiators like Amazon fulfillment) · Buy (configure a package, SaaS = subscription + vendor upgrades) · Integrate (keep ERP, add best-of-breed via middleware).

      PLAN: APS, S&OP, bullwhip, CPFR

      ERP MRPAPS
      CapacityInfinite (checked afterwards)Finite (respected while planning)
      LogicSequential: MPS → MRP → CRPSimultaneous constraint optimization
      SpeedOvernight batchIn-memory, minutes; live what-if
      ScopeSingle plantEnd-to-end network
      OutputPlan that may be infeasibleFeasible plan with trade-offs visible

      APS = simultaneous, constraint-based optimization across the whole network. Pays off in bottleneck plants, many SKUs/changeovers, volatile demand.

      1 Data gathering→2 Demand review (unconstrained, sales + marketing)→3 Supply review (capacity, APS)→4 Pre-S&OP (finance costs gaps)→5 Executive S&OP (approve ONE plan)
      • S&OP is monthly, plans product families (not detailed scheduling, which is the MPS). “One company, one set of numbers.” IBP ties it to finance and strategy.
      • Bullwhip: ±5% consumer swings → ±50% manufacturer swings. It is an information problem, so information systems cure it:
      CauseDigital cure
      Demand signal processingShare POS data, VMI, supplier portals (Walmart Retail Link)
      Order batchingEDI / auto-replenishment (cheaper small orders), TMS consolidation
      Price fluctuationEDLP, shared promotion calendars (CPFR)
      Shortage gamingAllocate on past sales, share capacity data

      CPFR (VICS, now GS1 US; Walmart + Warner-Lambert Listerine pilot): strategy & planning → demand & supply management → execution → analysis. Shares POS, store inventory, promo calendars, forecasts (EDI 830), exceptions. Needs trust; best for strategic partners and key SKUs.

      SOURCE: P2P, spend, TCO, SRM, selection

      1 Requisition2 Approval3 PO (EDI 850)4 Goods receipt5 Invoice (EDI 810)6 3-way match7 Payment
      • Three-way match: PO 100 × $10, receipt 95, invoice 100 × $10 → fails on quantity; block or pay $950. 2-way (PO vs invoice, services) · 3-way (goods) · 4-way (adds quality inspection). Tolerances (±2%) let small variances pass.
      • E-procurement platforms (SAP Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, Oracle): sourcing events (RFI, RFQ, reverse auctions), catalogs & guided buying, CLM, supplier portal, spend analytics, risk & performance.
      • Spend cube = supplier × category × business unit (+ time). Finds consolidation, maverick spend (off-contract), tail spend (many tiny suppliers), risk exposure.
      • TCO in sourcing: Supplier A quotes $6.00 but totals $8.80 (freight/duties, carrying, quality, risk); B quotes $7.20 and totals $7.85. Cheapest quote ≠ lowest cost.
      • SRM segments: transactional (price, commodities, auctions) → collaborative (shared forecasts, scorecards) → strategic partnership (co-development, long-term, ESG & risk co-management). Kraljic matrix. Apple Supplier Responsibility.
      • Selection: RFI → RFQ/RFP → evaluate (weighted criteria) → award (contract in CLM, vendor master). Scorecard: OTIF, PPM = defects ÷ units × 1,000,000 (500 PPM = 0.05%), lead-time variability (drives safety stock more than the average), responsiveness.
      • Weighted decision matrix: pick criteria → agree weights (=100%) before bids → score 1–5 with anchors → score × weight, sum → sensitivity-check. Packaging example: B wins at 3.80 vs C 3.70 vs A 3.65 (A is cheapest).
      • B2B ordering: EDI is today's default; portals complement; blockchain needs whole-network adoption (TradeLens shut 2023). Maersk digital booking.

      ORDER: CRM, OMS, O2C, promising

      • Supply chain cares about CRM: demand signals → PLAN, order visibility, returns start there.

      Receive (ASN vs PO)InspectPut away (slotting)Store (bins, cycle counts)Pick (waves, RF/voice)PackShip (send ASN)
      • WMS KPIs: pick accuracy >99.5%; dock-to-stock same day. Labour management across all steps.
      • Data capture: barcode (cheap, line-of-sight), RF handheld (real-time direction), RFID (many tags, no line of sight), voice (hands-free). Cloud vs on-premise; SAP EWM vs standalone; SMB: Fishbowl, Odoo.

      TRANSFORM & ORCHESTRATE: MES, OEE, SCEM

      ISA-95 levelSystemTime scale
      L4ERP, business planningDays / weeks

      • ERP sends work orders, BOMs, routings down; MES returns completions, consumption, scrap, quality. If scrap isn't reported, inventory is overstated and MRP under-orders.
      • OEE = Availability × Performance × Quality. 90% × 85% × 98% ≈ 75%; world-class ≈ 85%. Attack the weakest factor (here performance).
      • SCEM = manage by exception: Sense → Detect & alert → Decide → Act → Learn. Define event, threshold, owner, escalation. Metrics: MTTD (mean time to detect), resolution time.
      • Control tower = SCEM + unified visibility + analytics/AI; like air traffic control. Maturity: visibility → insight → action.

      Selecting technology

      1 Requirements (MoSCoW)→2 Fit-gap→3 Evaluate vendors (weighted matrix, scripted demos)→4 PoC / pilot→5 Business case→6 Implementation plan
      • Functional (what it must do) vs technical (how: cloud, APIs, security). MoSCoW: Must, Should, Could, Won't.
      • Fit = use as delivered · Configure = settings, no code · Gap = change process, add-on, workaround, or customize (last resort).
      • Business case: $1.2M investment, $450K/yr benefits → payback 2.7 yrs; 3-yr ROI = (1.35M − 1.2M) ÷ 1.2M = 12.5%.
      • Readiness: process documented, master data clean, integration ready, sponsor, change management.
      • Hershey 1999 (SAP R/3 + Manugistics + Siebel, compressed, big bang in July): ~$100M orders unfilled, profit down ~19%. Lessons: no peak go-live, end-to-end testing, phasing, fallback. Hershey = timing/testing/integration; Target Canada = master data.
